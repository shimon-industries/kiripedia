The **Federal Torture Act**, as [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) described it, was in force well before the September 11 attacks and had concrete enforcement history. Following World War II, the United States executed Japanese soldiers who had [waterboarded](/wiki/waterboarding) American POWs — establishing waterboarding as a death-penalty offense under U.S. law. Kiriakou has posed the resulting contradiction as a rhetorical question: *"Why in 1946 did we execute Japanese soldiers who had waterboarded American prisoners of war?"* — a death-penalty offense in 1946 that the CIA treated as legal in 2002.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="23:37" /><Cite s="2018-06-05-tedx-foggybottom-how-i-became-a-cia-whistleblower" t="8:31" />

In January 1968, when a *Washington Post* photograph showed a U.S. soldier waterboarding a North Vietnamese prisoner, Secretary of Defense Robert McNamara ordered an immediate investigation. The soldier was arrested, convicted of torture, and sentenced to twenty years at Leavenworth.<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="24:07" />

Kiriakou juxtaposed this enforcement history with the post-9/11 [CIA](/wiki/cia) program: *"But then in 2002, like magic, it's all legal."*<Cite s="2025-06-04-tucker-carlson-cia-torture-mk-ultra-911" t="24:07" /> He stresses the statute itself was never touched: Congress never voted on any change to the Federal Torture Act of 1946, and the text was never amended. What changed was only the Justice Department's own interpretation of it — Office of Legal Counsel opinions reclassified specific techniques as not constituting torture, making the practice legal in the executive branch's own reading without any actual legislative act.<Cite s="2024-08-12-danny-jones-loyal-officer-vs-dissident-spy" t="13:43" /> He traces the statute's roots to Nuremberg: passed in 1946, the same year the U.S. executed the Japanese soldiers, it sits alongside the United Nations Convention Against Torture — a convention Kiriakou says the United States itself authored and is a signatory to — as the two legal bases on which he concluded the CIA's post-9/11 program was a crime.<Cite s="2019-03-14-salem-access-tv-public" t="03:12" /><Cite s="2017-05-26-nicole-sandler" t="44:23" />

## 'The lawyers stood on their heads' (The Inquiry)

[John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) says torture is illegal *"by any measure"* under the [Federal Torture Act](/wiki/federal-torture-act) of 1946 and the United Nations Convention Against Torture, and that Justice Department lawyers *"stood on their heads to somehow make it not illegal"* after 9/11.<Cite s="2026-03-01-the-inquiry-torture-assassinations-epstein" t="01:38" /><Cite s="2026-03-01-the-inquiry-torture-assassinations-epstein" t="02:10" />

## Applied to Mitchell and Jessen's civil case

Kiriakou has invoked the statute directly against [Mitchell and Jessen](/wiki/mitchell-and-jessen), the CIA's contract psychologists, saying they are fortunate that the lawsuit brought against them over their torture of [Abu Zubaydah](/wiki/abu-zubaydah) and other detainees was only civil rather than criminal, since their conduct is a violation of both the Federal Torture Act of 1946 and the UN Convention Against Torture.<Cite s="2017-08-08-democracy-now-jeff-sessions-is-extending-obama-s-war-on-lea" t="13:56" />

## The line he says he drew in 2002

Kiriakou distinguishes sharply between the lawbreaking his job consisted of and the lawbreaking he refused. *"When you''re a CIA officer, your job is to break the law. It''s to break the laws of other countries. I was thrilled to get on a plane, go overseas and commit espionage. That''s what I was paid to do, and it was fun and it was rewarding. But I drew the line where it came to breaking US law."*<Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="20:58" /><Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="21:29" />

The objection he says he made to his superiors in the middle of 2002 rested on two instruments: a domestic statute *"which specifically prohibits us from doing exactly the things that you''re telling me we''re going to start doing,"* and the international convention — *"not only were we signatories to the United Nations Convention Against Torture, we wrote the United Nations Convention Against Torture."* In this telling he dates the domestic statute to 1946; the Federal Torture Act was enacted in 1994, and the discrepancy is preserved here as spoken.<Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="21:29" /><Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="22:00" />

## Why the case against the psychologists was only civil

Discussing [the Salim lawsuit](/wiki/salim-v-mitchell) in 2017, Kiriakou treats the choice of forum as the defendants' good fortune rather than a judgement on the conduct. *"I think frankly that Mitchell and Jessen are very, very fortunate in that this is not a criminal trial, because this is a violation — their actions are a violation of the federal torture act and a violation of the United Nations Convention Against Torture. So they're lucky, I think, that this is only a civil suit."* The year he gives for the statute, 1946, is preserved as spoken.<Cite s="2017-08-08-democracy-now-jeff-sessions-is-extending-obama-s-war-on-lea" t="13:56" />
