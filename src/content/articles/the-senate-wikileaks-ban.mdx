**The Senate WikiLeaks ban** is [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou)'s account of how the legislature responded to the publication of the [Chelsea Manning](/wiki/chelsea-manning) cables. He was then the senior investigator on the [Senate Foreign Relations Committee](/wiki/senate-foreign-relations-committee) staff and watched it happen from inside.<Cite s="2022-05-04-covertaction-bulletin-julian-assange-and-the-u-s-war-on-whistleblow" t="16:26" />

> *"The Senate security officer went from office to office to office — it took him days to go to the office of every one of the hundred senators and then every one of the committees and subcommittees — and said you may not access any of this information on a US Senate computer. It would constitute the crime of unauthorized access of classified information."*

The instruction was backed by an explicit threat: *"they threatened everybody with prosecution, and so nobody dared go to the WikiLeaks website."* The prohibition reached only the desks. *"Well, of course we're all news hounds, so we all watched the news coverage and we see exactly what it was that was released."*<Cite s="2022-05-04-covertaction-bulletin-julian-assange-and-the-u-s-war-on-whistleblow" t="16:26" /><Cite s="2022-05-04-covertaction-bulletin-julian-assange-and-the-u-s-war-on-whistleblow" t="16:56" />

## What he says was actually in it

Kiriakou's own assessment of the tranche, made from the coverage rather than from the documents, is deflationary. Ninety-nine per cent of it, he says, was *"just regular normal diplomatic cables. They meant nothing. This diplomat went to the foreign ministry and delivered a démarche or a white paper, or we asked this country to support our nominee for the international board of fisheries, or whatever — just normal diplomatic nonsense."*<Cite s="2022-05-04-covertaction-bulletin-julian-assange-and-the-u-s-war-on-whistleblow" t="16:56" />

The remainder is where he locates the argument: *"one percent of it was interesting, important, exposed crimes."* On the standing objection he is unequivocal, and treats the passage of time as the evidence: *"Did it harm the national security? I think we can say definitively after all these years — absolutely not."*<Cite s="2022-05-04-covertaction-bulletin-julian-assange-and-the-u-s-war-on-whistleblow" t="17:33" />

From which he draws the conclusion he repeats three times across the interview: *"the American people have the right to know what the government is doing in their name, and if what they're doing is against the law — a violation of either US or international law — not only do the American people have the right to know, but those crimes ought to be prosecuted."*<Cite s="2022-05-04-covertaction-bulletin-julian-assange-and-the-u-s-war-on-whistleblow" t="17:33" /><Cite s="2022-05-04-covertaction-bulletin-julian-assange-and-the-u-s-war-on-whistleblow" t="18:07" />
