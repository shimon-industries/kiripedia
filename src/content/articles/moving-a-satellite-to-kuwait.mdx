**Moving a satellite to Kuwait** is the anecdote [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) uses to convey how limited overhead reconnaissance was at the start of his career.

After Iraq [invaded Kuwait](/wiki/kuwait-invasion-intelligence) on 2 August 1990, the United States needed satellite coverage of the country and did not have it — because it had never needed it. Kiriakou says it took six months to move a satellite that had been sitting over Moscow so that it could show Kuwait.<Cite s="2025-11-17-dead-drop-s1e4-good-spies-bad-endings" t="43:16" />

He offers it in response to an account of the search for [Bill Buckley](/wiki/bill-buckley), the CIA's Beirut station chief kidnapped in 1984: a young analyst from the National Photographic Interpretation Center would take the day's single satellite image of Beirut and go over the rooftops with a magnifying glass, trying to match them against structures described in hostage debriefings.<Cite s="2025-11-17-dead-drop-s1e4-good-spies-bad-endings" t="42:41" /> Kiriakou's reply is that he can only imagine the work involved — and both men reach the same summary at the same moment: *"there was no Google Earth."*<Cite s="2025-11-17-dead-drop-s1e4-good-spies-bad-endings" t="43:16" />

## Why there was nothing overhead

The gap was not an oversight but a consequence of the alliance. Kiriakou, then a junior analyst, remembers the problem from inside: *"Satellites were very difficult to move and we didn't have any satellites over Kuwait. Why would we?"* — and answers his own question: *"We didn't have any satellites looking at Kuwait because we were friends with Kuwait. So we had to move one of our satellites from the Soviet Union."*<Cite s="2026-02-26-bidoun-waraq-invasion-kuwait-intelligence" t="32:00" />

## The six months of the war

The wait is the part of the story he treats as consequential, and he states the causal link directly: *"That's why we didn't liberate Kuwait until February of 1991. The Iraqis had invaded August of 1990, cuz we had to move a satellite from over Moscow all the way over to be over Iraq and Kuwait. And it took months to move the thing."* In that telling he widens the figure, giving the general case as six to twelve months to move a satellite from one place to another rather than the six he gives elsewhere.<Cite s="2026-04-06-danny-jones-iran-war-trumps-nightmare" t="2:39:15" /><Cite s="2026-04-06-danny-jones-iran-war-trumps-nightmare" t="2:39:47" />

He gives the same sequence in a co-constructed exchange with an interviewer: the satellite *"had to move in slow motion from over Moscow to over Kuwait. And once it was in place, then we were like, 'Okay, now we can do it.'"*<Cite s="2026-02-26-bidoun-waraq-invasion-kuwait-intelligence" t="32:30" /> The contrast he draws with the present is the same one he draws in the Buckley story: it took that long *"back then. Now you just go to Google Earth and just look, you know, one day old photographs, but it was a different world back then."*<Cite s="2026-02-26-bidoun-waraq-invasion-kuwait-intelligence" t="32:30" />

Kiriakou also uses the constraint as the setup for a different lesson. Introducing an example of a false flag *"that we studied at the agency"*, he begins from the same premise — that before Google Earth it could take six to twelve months to reposition overhead coverage, and that some countries had no satellites at all — before turning to the Israeli interest in the port of Benghazi.<Cite s="2026-04-06-danny-jones-iran-war-trumps-nightmare" t="2:39:15" /><Cite s="2026-04-06-danny-jones-iran-war-trumps-nightmare" t="2:39:47" />

The same absence shaped his own tradecraft in the field, where [planning a surveillance detection route](/wiki/planning-a-surveillance-detection-route) meant photocopying road-atlas pages and taping them together, and where the [hunt for Abu Zubaydah](/wiki/mr-fish) in Pakistan was conducted with 1961 maps.<Cite s="2025-12-29-dead-drop-s1e8-the-james-bond-academy" t="13:30" /><Cite s="2026-02-23-dead-drop-s1e16-captured" t="07:31" />
