**Kompromat** is a Russian-derived term for compromising material — recordings, photographs, or documentation of an individual's criminal, sexual, or embarrassing behavior — used to coerce cooperation or silence. [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) has described [CIA](/wiki/cia) experience with kompromat operations and the agency's formal policy conclusion about their effectiveness.

## Failed 1970s operation

Kiriakou described a [CIA](/wiki/cia) kompromat operation conducted against a foreign cleric in the 1970s. The CIA arranged for a prostitute to sleep with the target, photographed the encounter, and approached him with the material, expecting to recruit him as an asset. The cleric's response, in Kiriakou's telling: *"Go ahead and publish them. I'll tell everybody you're lying. Give me eight-by-tens and wallet sizes."*<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="15:00" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="15:30" />

The operation produced nothing. The target was not recruited.<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="15:30" />

## CIA policy: coercion doesn't work

Kiriakou stated that on the basis of operations like this one, the [CIA](/wiki/cia) arrived at a formal policy position: coercion and blackmail do not work as recruitment tools. His summary of the underlying logic: when you approach someone with kompromat, you are forcing a binary choice — *"You either confess to your wife, or you commit treason. Which do you pick?"*<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="16:00" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="16:30" />

In Kiriakou's assessment, most people — when faced with that choice — confess. The target absorbs a short-term personal consequence rather than a lifetime of espionage. The [CIA](/wiki/cia) concluded that voluntary, ideologically or financially motivated recruitment produces far more reliable assets than coerced ones.<Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="16:30" /><Cite s="2026-05-05-morgan-nelson-cia-whistleblower-control-anyone" t="17:00" /> Kiriakou puts a figure on it in a separate interview: internal CIA studies found the agency stopped blackmailing sources in the 1970s because it proved less effective than voluntary cooperation, with roughly 95% of CIA source relationships built on cash and only about 5% driven by ideology, excitement, or revenge.<Cite s="2026-03-10-former-congressman-matt-ga" t="38:01" />

## An intern's first day

Kiriakou has separately described his own first CIA boss recounting a formative episode from his own earliest days at the agency, as a grad-school intern: on his first day, warned by the office secretary never to look inside a wall of file folders in the room, he looked anyway — and found that every single file was on an American citizen.<Cite s="2026-02-13-o-keefe-media-group" t="1:05:48" />

## Why the agency stopped

Kiriakou says the question reaches him constantly — *"people raise this with me literally every single day"* — and his answer is operational rather than ethical. *"The CIA doesn''t blackmail. We used to, but they haven''t done that since the ''70s, because it''s not effective. In order to really squeeze great information out of a source, the source has to love you. The source has to think that you''re his best friend."* The relationship he describes is transactional underneath and affectionate on the surface at the same time: the source knows he is being paid, *"but really it''s because you''re in a bromance and he loves hanging out with you, and he''s going to give you the plans to the next-generation Russian tank just because he loves you."*<Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="37:05" /><Cite s="2026-02-27-one-america-news-network-one-america-news-network" t="37:37" />
