**The U.S.–Saudi relationship** is described by [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou) as the keystone of American foreign policy in the Middle East — a judgement he offers *"for better or for worse"* rather than as praise.<Cite s="2015-09-23-mintpress-news-former-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-takes-us-insid" t="02:41" />

## What each side gets

The American interest he names first is basing. It is always useful to have friends in the region, he says, especially when you find yourself in a time of war and suddenly need air bases or naval bases.<Cite s="2015-09-23-mintpress-news-former-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-takes-us-insid" t="02:41" />

Beyond that he describes the arrangement as unusually uncomplicated: *"we buy their oil and they buy our military hardware. It's really that simple."* Outside that exchange, he says, the two governments generally agree on very little in terms of policy.<Cite s="2015-09-23-mintpress-news-former-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-takes-us-insid" t="03:13" />

## What it commits the United States to

Against the basing rights he sets the character of the client. The United States has committed itself for well more than half a century to defending what he calls an extreme right-wing government with no respect for human rights, no respect for women's rights, and no respect for religious freedoms.<Cite s="2015-09-23-mintpress-news-former-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-takes-us-insid" t="03:13" />

The commitment, in his account, is to the regime's survival rather than to any shared position — and it is honoured at the expense of the stated principles of American policy: *"we have proven time and again that we will walk away from our own commitments to international law and to human rights in order to defend and protect that regime."*<Cite s="2015-09-23-mintpress-news-former-cia-agent-john-kiriakou-takes-us-insid" t="03:45" />
