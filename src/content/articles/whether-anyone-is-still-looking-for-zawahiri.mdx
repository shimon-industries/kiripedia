The subject came up as an aside during a discussion of the hunt for al-Qaeda's leadership. Asked about the man described as al-Qaeda's number two, still at large, and still unfound, Kiriakou did not offer an operational explanation.<Cite s="2019-12-23-one-tough-podcast-with-bo-dietl-episode-76-john-kiriakou" t="27:10" />

## The disclaimer

He framed the answer with an explicit limit on his own authority. He had no inside information here, he said; he was speaking as an educated observer.<Cite s="2019-12-23-one-tough-podcast-with-bo-dietl-episode-76-john-kiriakou" t="27:10" /> The distinction is one he draws often, and it is doing real work in this instance: he had left the agency fifteen years earlier and was reasoning from public facts.

## The suggestion

What he wondered was whether the United States was simply not looking for Zawahiri any more.<Cite s="2019-12-23-one-tough-podcast-with-bo-dietl-episode-76-john-kiriakou" t="27:10" />

The reason he gave was a judgment about relevance rather than about capability. Al-Qaeda, he said, is pretty much irrelevant now.<Cite s="2019-12-23-one-tough-podcast-with-bo-dietl-episode-76-john-kiriakou" t="27:10" /> Attention had gone elsewhere, to ISIS, and maybe other groups, and the possibility he raised was that the Americans just did not care about al-Qaeda any more. *"I wonder if we just walked away."*<Cite s="2019-12-23-one-tough-podcast-with-bo-dietl-episode-76-john-kiriakou" t="27:43" />

## The shape of the claim

Kiriakou did not assert that the search had been abandoned, and he did not claim knowledge of a decision. He offered an inference from an absence: an organisation that had been the object of the largest manhunt in American history had produced no result against its surviving leader for eighteen years, at a time when the strategic case for the hunt had visibly weakened.

It is a characteristic move in his commentary, treating the allocation of intelligence effort, rather than the competence of it, as the thing that explains an outcome. He has made the same argument about analytic failure elsewhere: that what an agency looks at is a policy choice, and that the choice is rarely announced.
