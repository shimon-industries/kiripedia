The **prison service economy** is [John Kiriakou](/wiki/john-kiriakou)'s account of the trade that ran alongside the [food economy](/wiki/prison-food-economy) at [FCI Loretto](/wiki/fci-loretto): *"there was plenty of room for entrepreneurs."*<Cite s="2026-05-25-dead-drop-s2e2-rules-of-the-game" t="06:49" />

## The trades

One man made a decent living reselling other prisoners' used shoes, old pairs no longer worn, or shoes offloaded by men about to be released, taking a commission on each sale; Kiriakou bought two pairs from him. Others ran convenience stores out of their lockers, selling sodas, sweets and crisps at a standard thirty per cent markup, the same margin as the [commissary](/wiki/prison-commissary) itself.<Cite s="2026-05-25-dead-drop-s2e2-rules-of-the-game" t="06:49" />

There were a dozen bookmakers at Loretto, a good friend of his the most successful of them, and some prisoners made thousands of dollars a month from gambling, the wise ones sending it home to build a nest egg for release. Others repaired radios, did unofficial and unlicensed chiropractic work, or sold underwear, socks and t-shirts stolen from the laundry.<Cite s="2026-05-25-dead-drop-s2e2-rules-of-the-game" t="07:21" />

## The currency

The unit of account was the book of postage stamps, which sold at the commissary for $8.20 and traded inside at a flat seven dollars. Kiriakou explains the preference in practical terms: unlike seven bags of mackerel stacked one on another, a book of stamps is small, easy to hide and easy to spend.<Cite s="2026-05-25-dead-drop-s2e2-rules-of-the-game" t="07:53" />

He is explicit about what the most enterprising prisoners sold for that currency, including sexual services with a published price list, and records one man selling his *"turnkey business"* and its dozen clients for sixty-five books of stamps.<Cite s="2026-05-25-dead-drop-s2e2-rules-of-the-game" t="07:53" /><Cite s="2026-05-25-dead-drop-s2e2-rules-of-the-game" t="08:24" />

## His own position

Kiriakou did not participate. Prison work paid him $1.08 a month, which he banked, relying on family and friends for anything he needed, though he adds that had he needed capitalism in prison, it was there.<Cite s="2026-05-25-dead-drop-s2e2-rules-of-the-game" t="08:24" />

The same economy explains what was on the plate. Kitchen workers stole chicken, beef and pork to order for customers whose families then transferred $100 to $150 a month into the cook's commissary account, which for everyone else meant *"chickenless chicken pot pie"*, hot dogs instead of roast beef and sloppy joes instead of pork loin, almost none of it human-grade food.<Cite s="2026-05-25-dead-drop-s2e2-rules-of-the-game" t="06:17" />
