Never meet my grandchildren
===========================

Describing the period between his arrest in January 2012 and his plea, John Kiriakou has given an account of the pressure applied to him that begins not with the charges but with what other people in his position do under them.

The framing

"A lot of whistleblowers commit suicide," he said, "because the pressure is so great." He then set out his own exposure: he was facing forty-five years in prison on the original charges. He repeats the figure, "Forty-five years in prison", as a sentence of its own.

The threat

What followed is the specific remark he has singled out from the whole prosecution. "One of the prosecutors told me that I was going to die in prison and that I would never meet my grandchildren."

He does not name the prosecutor, give a date, or say whether the remark was made in a negotiation, a filing or a corridor. It is offered without elaboration, as the thing that was said to him.

What came next

The passage turns immediately from the threat to the counterweight. His wife at the time, "now my ex-wife", told him he had to embrace it: he had done nothing wrong, and so he had to keep talking about it, because his side of the story needed to become the side of record. He judges that she was right, and says he began giving interviews to every outlet in the world that asked, never saying no.

Placing it

The forty-five-year figure is one Kiriakou has used consistently, and the arithmetic of how it came down first to ten years and then to a plea he could live with is recorded elsewhere. What this passage adds is the human end of the same process: a threat aimed not at his liberty but at his family, delivered by someone on the other side of the table, and remembered more than a decade later as the thing worth quoting.
