The quarterback with a personal chef
====================================

The quarterback with a personal chef is John Kiriakou's illustration of the proposition that money buys different treatment inside a federal prison, told on the DeProgram programme in July 2025 in response to a viewer who wrote that "rich people get different rules, even in prison." Kiriakou's reply was that this was "absolutely true", and he offered the man in the next row of cells as proof.

Who he was

Kiriakou could not retrieve the name, "I can't remember his name", but gives the identifying details: "he was a quarterback for the New York Giants in the early 1970s," serving "like 25 years for running this major massive Ponzi scheme." Kiriakou's estimate of the economics is arithmetical rather than documentary: "let's say he made a hundred million and they made him pay 50 million in restitution. He still had 50 million left over." He does not soften his opinion of the man, calling him "a real scumbag."

The protection payments

The surplus was put to work. "What this guy did was he paid off all the Mexican and black gangs to ensure his safety," Kiriakou says, giving figures the man himself supplied: "he paid like $500 a month, I think, is what he told me, like $500 a month to the MS-13 shot caller, 500 to the Crips, 500 to the Bloods, 500 to the Mexican Mafia."

The kitchen

The second arrangement was culinary. Kiriakou notes that hatred of the cafeteria food was universal, "he hated the food in the cafeteria. Everybody did", but that only this prisoner did anything structural about it: "he hired one of the Hispanics to be his personal chef."

The supply chain ran through an economy Kiriakou says the staff tolerated. Stealing food from the cafeteria was a standard side hustle, and "they're allowed to steal food. The guards look the other way." The quarterback therefore "would spend his 500 a month in the commissary that he was allowed to spend, and then he would buy the rest of it on the black market stolen from the cafeteria. And he had this personal chef make him three meals a day."

The conclusion

Kiriakou drew the moral himself, agreeing with the viewer who had prompted the story: "it's good to be king ... Rich people are different. There are different rules even in prison." Asked immediately afterwards how the same system had treated Martha Stewart, he answered that everybody had loved her, and that her experience had been "kind of like mine", a comparison he grounds in still being in touch with the Italians he served alongside.
