Kiriakou's Greek citizenship
============================

Kiriakou's Greek citizenship dates from 2010. John Kiriakou is the grandson of immigrants from Greece and grew up in a Greek-American household in western Pennsylvania, fluent enough in the language that his Greek was, as he puts it, "a given" by the time the CIA needed someone who spoke both Greek and Arabic.

How it was offered

He describes acquiring the nationality with evident pride: "so I acquired Greek citizenship and I am proud to say that I am a Greek national, a Greek citizen." The offer, in his telling, came from the Greek state rather than the other way round. When his trouble with the U.S. government began, the Greek ambassador approached him and asked what could be done to be helpful. Kiriakou answered that he could be given Greek citizenship, and he was.

The receptiveness had a cause. Greeks have always been fascinated by the CIA, and here was a Greek-American CIA officer who had made good, blown the whistle and taken it on the chin because he wanted to do the right thing and tell the truth, a national hero in Greece for having stood up to the American government. Greek newspapers came to the prison so often he lost count. His children are Greek citizens too; the family holds dual nationality.

What it was worth

Kiriakou calls it the thing that really saved him, and is explicit that this is luck no other whistleblower had. The years after prison were bad: he bounced from job to job, ran crowdfunding campaigns to pay his own salary and could not clear minimum wage, took a radio-hosting job that suited him and carried the best health insurance of his life but not enough money to live on, and his wife left. He was in his mid-fifties, starting over.

What the passport produced was work. The Greek government hired him to write a new whistleblower protection law; he travelled to Greece five times in 2015, and the law was drafted and presented.

It also produced one spectacular misfire. In August 2020 he was offered what he calls the job that dropped out of heaven into his lap and his big comeback, chief operating officer of a private equity firm based in Athens, for more money than he could ever have dreamt of. He took it gladly. Six weeks in he realized it was a money laundering operation, resigned, and called the FBI.

The passport is not merely sentimental in smaller ways either. Discussing travel restrictions he has faced as an American felon, Kiriakou notes that he could have gone through Cancún on his Greek passport anyway, "so it was no skin off my nose."

Divided loyalty

The timing matters for one recurring question put to him. Asked about divided loyalty, the charge he levels at Americans he considers primarily loyal to another state, Kiriakou's answer is that he was born in the United States and became a dual citizen only in 2010, decades after he began serving the American government.
