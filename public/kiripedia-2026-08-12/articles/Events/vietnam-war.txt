Vietnam War
===========

The Vietnam War predates John Kiriakou's career, he was born in 1964, and his parents marched against the war, but it supplies the precedents for nearly every argument he makes.

The war of lies

Kiriakou files Vietnam first in his catalogue of wars launched on falsehoods: the push for U.S. involvement came from the Gulf of Tonkin incident, "a lie", just as Iraq came from lies later. The Pentagon Papers showed not only that the war was being lost but that the generals had known it for almost a decade while lying to the president, which is why Kiriakou calls Daniel Ellsberg "the godfather of all modern national security whistleblowers." He repeats the consensus tribute without irony: Ellsberg is "the guy that ended the Vietnam War." He even quotes the biographer's account of Lyndon Johnson explaining what the war was about by exposing himself.

The torture precedent

Vietnam anchors Kiriakou's core case against torture: American POWs have described being tortured for names and answering with the starting lineup of the Green Bay Packers, or inventing ships and positions in the South China Sea, "literally anything that they think you want to hear just to get you to stop." Waterboarding was used as recently as Vietnam, part of a lineage running back through the Belgians in the Congo to medieval China. And the era's own villain knew better: even Robert McNamara, "the one responsible for the Vietnam War", understood the technique was too clearly illegal.

The institutional reckoning

In Kiriakou's history of the CIA, Vietnam marks the turn: the agency "ran wild" unchecked from 1947 through the war's end, until 1975, the war lost, the assassination programs exposed, when things finally tipped toward the Church Committee era of oversight. The Cold War's worst nightmare, he says flatly, was the U.S. invasion of Vietnam.
