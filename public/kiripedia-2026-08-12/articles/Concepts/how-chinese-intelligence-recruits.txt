How Chinese intelligence recruits
=================================

How Chinese intelligence recruits is John Kiriakou's general assessment of the Ministry of State Security's agent-handling, given on 19 November 2025 alongside his own account of being approached and in response to British allegations that the service was working through professional networking sites.

Where they are good, and where they are not

Kiriakou draws the line at whether the target knows what he is doing. "The Chinese are successful when they're trying to recruit somebody who just wants money. They don't care about the fallout. They don't care about the risks. They want money. The Chinese have the money."

The developmental case, the slow cultivation of someone who has not yet understood who is on the other end, is, in his judgement, where they fail. "In terms of developing a source who may or may not realise he's being recruited by Chinese intelligence, they're really bad at it. They're really, really bad at it."

What they are usually after is not political. "Normally, they're looking for people who have access to very sophisticated technology."

The progression, and the exception

Asked whether the method is the familiar one, begin by requesting a white paper or a report that is already public, then ask for a little more, Kiriakou confirmed it: "normally that would be the progression, yes."

But he identified a category of target for whom the public-document stage is beside the point. "With a member of parliament, you would also have access to inside information on official deliberations. Anything from trade to defence to politics, you would have an insider reporting back to you."

He then priced that access precisely. "Now listen, 90 per cent of the time you'll be able to get that same information out of a newspaper. But that extra 10 per cent, they're willing to pay good money for it." And on the ceiling: "Money's no object. They're flush with money."

The defence

Asked what a targeted country can actually do, Kiriakou offered no technical remedy, only training and instinct. "Teach your people good counterintelligence techniques. Most people wouldn't realise that they were being pitched or targeted by a Chinese intelligence officer, and that's the whole point from the Chinese side."

The residual protection is the oldest one in the trade: "just have your counterintelligence antenna up," and treat a gut objection as decisive rather than embarrassing.

He also flagged the obvious difficulty with pattern-matching on names, when the host observed that the recruiters in the British case were plainly Chinese: "the problem is, there's lots of legitimate Chinese business people."
