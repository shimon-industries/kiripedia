Letters the cabinet ignored
===========================

Letters the cabinet ignored is John Kiriakou's summary of what congressional oversight correspondence actually achieved during his years on the staff of the Senate Foreign Relations Committee. He offered it on the DeProgram programme in July 2025, asked whether the Department of Homeland Security was obliged to answer a demand from two minority-party House members for its internal policies on how immigration agents should dress and conduct themselves.

The formal answer

Kiriakou's answer to the narrow question was no. The conduct of the department was, he said, "an executive order function. They don't have to reply."

The practical answer

He then generalised from experience. "One of the things I learned when I was on Capitol Hill at the Senate Foreign Relations Committee is most of the time cabinet members just ignore us," he says. The letters went out at every level of seniority: "We would write letters to the secretary of whatever, or assistant secretary, or the FBI director or the CIA director."

The result did not vary. "They would just ignore us."

Why nothing followed

What interests Kiriakou is the absence of any consequence, which he explains in terms of who had actually signed. "And what are you going to do? Is it worth going to the mat over the fact that they ignored the letter from your staff member? No."

The implication he draws is that the oversight instrument is weakest precisely where it is used most: routine written enquiries, issued at staff level, carry no cost for the official who bins them, and escalating each one would exhaust the committee's leverage on trivia.

His forecast for the 2025 request followed directly: "I don't think they're going to get any satisfaction here."

The remark is one of a small set in which Kiriakou describes the oversight system from the congressional side rather than the agency side, and its tenor matches what he says about the agency side, that the reporting relationship is formally strict and practically discretionary.
